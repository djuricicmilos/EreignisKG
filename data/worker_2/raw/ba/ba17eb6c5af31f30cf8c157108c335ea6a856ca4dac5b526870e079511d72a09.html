5. Those approaches presume the very thing we are trying to find. The ontic description of things in nature and things of value and the traditional interpretations of their being are dead ends when it comes to how we experience the world of meaning. The traditional approach is only about objects in their objective ways of being. They presuppose meaningfulness rather than directly investigating it.


64c = 92e = 64c

6. Some issues at this point. Even though those two approaches fail, we nonetheless do have a way to get an experience of the meaning-giving world. We said that things in nature and things of value are within the meaning-giving world and that the world of meaning is intrinsic to ex-sistence. But does that mean each of us has his or her own private world of meaning? If so, that would make the world of meaning subjective, and then what would happen to the common world of meaning that we all share? So, which one are we talking about: subjective worlds of meaning or common worlds of meaning? The answer: Neither one. This chapter focuses on the essential structure of any lived world of meaning. How do we experience that?


64d = 92f = 64d

7. Ex-sistence itself is the world of meaning. As one moment in our a priori involvement in meaning, the structure of the meaning-giving world is an ontological matter. The world of meaning is intrinsic to ex-sistence and is itself ex-sistential, so that in investigating that world, we are actually investigating our ex-sistence. Meaningfulness is an intrinsic element of ex-sistence alone and not of anything within the world of meaning. Nonetheless, we will begin by investigating non-ex-sistential things in terms of their way of being, something which is not as simple as it might seem. We start with some important distinctions.
