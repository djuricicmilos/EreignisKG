But the genius of Aristotle, as Heidegger likes to repeat,14 is precisely that he grasped movement as a kind of beingness (ἐνεργεια τις, 201b 30f.), the condition of a being which at once stands in its τέλος (en-tel-echeia) without having fully arrived there (tou dynatou hei dynaton, 201 b 5), the state of a being which Is presentin partial appearance, yet absent in relative non-appearance (ἐνεργεια ατελής, 201 b 32).

"Relative non-appearance"—that is, non-appearance for the sake of appearance—is what Heidegger takes Aristotle to mean by δύναμις; indeed, this δύναμις is the possibilizing condition for a moving being’s partial and negatived appearance. Precisely this "atelic" quality of a moving being is what allows it to remain in movement, for were the δύναμις brought forward into the telos, the being would be achieved and the movement would cease. This atelic presentness constitutes a unique interplay of presence and absence, for along with its limited presence, a moving being’s non-presence or possibilizing absence also becomes present in a special way. For example, the absence of Pierre from the café and of my stolen bicycle from the rack are indeed present and given, even when the absent beings are not. Likewise in a growing natural being, the relative non-appearance of the source of growth indirectly becomes present in allowing the flower to appear.15 To spell out δύναμις in terms of presence and relative absence is to lay out the genealogically most primitive form of “retrieve” (Wiederholung). Indeed if it may be said that Heidegger’s topic is the emergent topos (φύσις) and if, as Heidegger argues, φύσις is κίνησις or ἐνεργεια ατελής, and if this atelic presence has the fundamental structure of drawing δύναμις finitely into ἐνεργεια while ever allowing δύναμις its non-appearance, then we may begin to see anew the unity and simplicity in Heidegger’s thought precisely by discovering its Aristotelian roots.
