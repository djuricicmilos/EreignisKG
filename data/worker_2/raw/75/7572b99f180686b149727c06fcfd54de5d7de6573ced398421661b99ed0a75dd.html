It is one thing to metaphysically install being as beingness in a most-beingful [Seiendstes] (timiōtaton on [supreme being])—and another to experience beyng in terms of the abidance of its truth as “the most-beingful” in the sense that it is precisely not “a” being and also not supreme and not “the” absolute. (GA 67, 85)

Being3 “is most” precisely in terms of singular instantaneity and spatiotemporal situatedness. Being3 “is most” because it is “at times”—it is locality and instantaneity as such. “Beyng, and only beyng, is. But beyng is at times [zuzeiten], in that beyng itself, as the appropriation [Ereignung] of the ‘between’ [Inzwischen], lets the clearing [Lichtung] emerge as time-space [Zeit-Raum]” (GA 70, 15).

Beings “are” not, in the sense that they are only the explicit foreground aspect of the event of being3 that was cut off or de-cided by metaphysics from its implicit background dimensions (being2). This cutting-off pertains to the very foreground/background structure of being3 itself, to the fact that its foreground (being1) tends to become detached and considered in its own right. The unity of being3 is a differential, yet simple and ultimately indivisible, singular unity of foreground and background.

There is nothing in any realm that corresponds to the relation of beyng and beings—it is unique-singular [einzig-einmalig]. And the unity of the “distinct” ones [“Unterschiedenen”; i.e., that of beyng and beings] is the truth of “the” beyng itself into which unconcealed beings in each instance “abide.” (GA 69, 22)

The dictum “beyng is, beings are not” is an attempt to dissolve the persistent misinterpretation of the “ontological difference” as a relation between two relata, “being” and “beings,” and the widespread notion that Heidegger professes to study being instead of and apart from beings.30 The implausibility of this becomes obvious precisely when one realizes that what is distinguished from beings is not a being and thus cannot be a “member” or relatum of any relation. The final dictum of Contributions, as well as the later articulation of the ontological difference as reciprocal “discharge” (Austrag; see Chapter 6), is a directive to understand the ontological difference in terms of the differential, decisive, and detaching structure of being3 itself. The ontological difference, the coming apart of “beings” (presence, being1) and “being” (being2)—or, rather, the differentiation of being and the sense of being, of presence and nonpresence, of clearing and concealment—is ultimately a structure of being3 as event.
