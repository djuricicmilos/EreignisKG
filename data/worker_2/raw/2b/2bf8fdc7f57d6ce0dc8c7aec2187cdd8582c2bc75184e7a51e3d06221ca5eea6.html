Appearance-2: a mere semblance. Appearance-2, the secondary and non-genuine sense of phainomenon, is something that appears as what it is not. It is a semblance or specious appearance that merely looks like, or seems to be, this or that. In Aristotle, for example, a phainomenon agathon is “a mere semblance of the good.” o

It is crucial to understand the structural connection between the primary and the secondary meanings of phainomenon. Only because a thing can show up at all can it also show up as a specious appearance, i.e., as what it is not. In other words, the secondary sense presumes the primary one, and the primary sense always underlies the secondary one. To put it differently, the secondary sense is a privative modification of the primary one. Here we use the word “privative” in the sense of the Greek sterēsis. o By merely looking like a genuine appearance, a specious appearance “deprives” a genuine appearance of its genuineness.

We will reserve the term “phenomenon” for the first and genuine sense of the term, something that is manifest to us as it actually is, even if incompletely. For the secondary sense, we will use terms like “semblance” or “specious appearance” [der Schein]. We turn now to the third and fourth senses of “appearance” in order to contrast them with appearance-1.

7. Appearance-3: that through which something else indirectly appears. A symptom, e.g., a rash, is certainly a phenomenon: it does show up as what it is, namely as a rash. But the rash indicates something else connected with it: a physical disorder that is present in the body but does not directly show up. To the degree that it is a symptom, the rash is not a phenomenon in the genuine sense (an appearance-1). Like a non-genuine phenomenon or appearance-2, it does show up but does not show itself so much as indicating something else. However, the “not” + of an appearance-3 does not have the privative sense associated with the “not” of an appearance-2, a mere semblance, and that for two reasons.

  • A symptom neither hides the disease nor deprives the disease of an appearance. On the contrary, it manifests the disease.
  • A disease cannot show up directly as the disease it is; it always shows up only through symptoms.

In addition to symptoms, other examples of appearance-3 include symbols, indications, depictions, and the like. (See Figure 17.3.) All of these are species of the genus “something by means of which something else appears,” although of course they are distinguished by their specific differences. (Cf. Figure A-1.1.)
